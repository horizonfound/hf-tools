# Writing the hook (the on-screen line)

The hook is measured against the client, not against taste. Nicole's rules, in her words where she gave them.

- **It speaks to the client about what she feels and wants.** "Hooks speak to a mom, about what she feels and wants, not how Nicole works." The voice check's most-off line was "I barely directed this session. This is what happens when you just let them play." Rewritten: "You're always the one taking the picture. This time you're in it."
- **One short line.** Two at most, and the second one small. It has to read in a second on a phone with the sound off.
- **A real angle.** "Hooks need a real angle or strategy that feels unique to her, not just a nice line." If any photographer could post it, cut it.
- **Never mirror the spoken opener.** If the talking clip says it, the text says something else.
- **Never invent.** No client names without their OK, no dates, no prices, no offers that weren't given.
- **Photos first.** The hook earns its place on the opening; it does not stay on unless they ask (`--hook-persist`).
- **Ten to twenty options**, numbered. They pick one for the feed. Four or five more become trial reels of the same cut. The rest are gone.

Shapes that work for photographers (use the Brand File's phrases inside them):
1. The feeling she'll have: "You're always the one taking the picture. This time you're in it."
2. The season: "The last beach day with just the two of them."
3. What she didn't have to do: "She didn't pose once. Look what happened."
4. The moment: "The second she stopped fixing her hair."
5. The thing they'll say later: "Her mom cried at this one."
