# The Studio, concept brief v3 (Nicole's Claude, Oct 21 11:56pm)

Source: Slack #the-engine, file `The-Studio-Brief-v3.docx`. Written by Claude in Nicole's chat from the Oct 21 conversation; copied here unchanged so the repo has it. Tables from the original are flattened to lists.

## The idea

Every photographer gets a staff. The Studio Manager takes care of her files, backups and galleries. The Assistant turns her favorite images into reels, carousels, stories and captions. Later, the Concierge looks after her clients, from the first inquiry to the anniversary reminder a year later. They all live on her own computer, they all work from the same folders and the same Studio File, and they all speak in her voice.

She plugs in her card tonight and wakes up to it done. When the gallery is delivered, the Studio Manager hands her favorites to the Assistant, and by the time she has finished her coffee she has a reel cut on the beat, a carousel, stories and captions ready to post. Every creative decision stays with her, and everything around the art is handled by the team.

The tools photographers use today each speed up one step, such as culling, editing in their style, or sending automated emails, and the photographer still stitches those steps together by hand across five or six apps. The Studio is the first system that runs the whole job around the art, from the moment a card goes in to the post that books her next client.

## One product, not three

The staff works because they share everything: one install, one Studio File, one set of folders and one voice. The Studio Manager can only hand favorites to the Assistant, and the Concierge can only ask for a testimonial with the right photos attached, because they are part of the same system. Selling them separately would mean three setups and would break the handoff that makes the whole thing feel like magic.

Recommended structure. The Studio is a one-time digital product that the photographer buys, installs with the help of Nicole's walkthrough videos, and owns. It runs on her own computer using her own Claude plan, so Horizon Found carries no running cost each time it works. The Engine stays available on its own as the Assistant. The Studio adds the Studio Manager, with the two working together. Each new hire, starting with the Concierge, is sold as an add-on for Studio owners, which fits the way Horizon Found already sells with upsells and order bumps, and gives a new launch moment every time someone joins the staff.

## Meet the staff

- **Studio Manager** (launch): Backs up and checks every file, marks blinks and duplicates for review, sorts several sessions on one card into their own folders, sets up the gallery, checks the export, uploads, and hands favorites to the Assistant.
- **Assistant** (launch, already built): The Engine. Cuts reels to the beat of the song she picks, builds carousels from simple layouts, and writes hooks, captions and stories.
- **Concierge** (first new hire): Checks the inbox a few times a day, researches new inquiries, drafts replies, sends milestone reminders, asks for testimonials and files them with the photos, and suggests thank-you gifts.
- **Lighting Tech** (later hire): Reads a photo of a dark room and tells her where to bounce the flash. A natural companion to the Flash Course.
- **Creative Director** (later hire): Builds mood boards and helps her brainstorm ideas for upcoming shoots.

Photographers can give each member of their staff a name, so it feels like their own team and not a piece of software.

## What it feels like

**The night of a session.** She plugs in her card and says something like "Johnson family tonight, beach session, gallery due in two weeks." The Studio Manager copies everything to the drive she chose during setup, whether that is an external hard drive or another folder, then backs it up to her cloud drive and checks every file. It marks blinks and duplicates for her review, suggests three sneak peeks, drafts a note to the client, adds the delivery date to her calendar, and sets up an empty gallery with her usual settings.

**The next morning.** She opens one short report. It tells her the session is safe in two places, shows the sneak peek choices and a caption in her voice, and has the client note waiting for her approval. It takes about four minutes.

**Delivery day and the handoff.** She edits and exports the way she always does. The Studio Manager checks the export, uploads it to the gallery, sets the cover, confirms the count, and drafts the delivery email for her approval. Then it pulls her favorites into their own folder and asks the question that sums up the whole system:

"The Johnson gallery is delivered. Would you like me to pass the favorites to your Assistant for a reel, a carousel and stories?"

**It handles your real days, not just ideal ones.** The Studio Manager is built around how photographers actually work, so it adapts when a day doesn't fit the simple pattern of one session, one card. Mini session days are a good example. She might photograph eight families back to back on one card, then upload a screenshot of her booking calendar, and the Studio Manager uses the time each photo was taken to sort every family into its own named folder, with a separate client note, sneak peeks and captions for each. Multi-day shoots, two cards from one wedding, or a card holding three different sessions from the week work the same way: she tells it what happened, and it sorts the work out for her.

**Who picks the favorites.** Photographers trust their own eye, and they would not want software deciding what is good. So the favorites folder is filled by the photographer's own picks or by the client's favorites from the gallery. As an option she can turn on once she trusts it, the Studio Manager can suggest favorites and learn from the choices she makes over time.

## The Studio Manager's flow

- **Card goes in** — Copies every image to the drive she chose during setup, using her naming system, then backs up to her cloud drive. *Automatic.*
- **Busy days** — When one card holds several sessions, like a mini session day, it sorts each one into its own named folder. *She confirms.*
- **Right after** — Checks each copy against the original, so both backups are confirmed identical, not just counted. *Automatic.*
- **Right after** — Marks closed eyes, missed focus and near-duplicates for review. Nothing is moved or removed. *She confirms.*
- **Right after** — Suggests sneak peeks, drafts a client note, adds the delivery date to her calendar and sets up the gallery. *She chooses and approves.*
- **Next morning** — Shows everything on one page, with any problem at the very top. *She reviews.*
- **After she edits** — Checks the export, uploads, sets the cover, confirms the count and drafts the delivery email. *She approves.*
- **After delivery** — Pulls favorites into their own folder and offers to hand them to the Assistant. *She says yes.*

## Safety promises

Images can never be reshot, so the Studio Manager is built around one promise: the worst thing that can happen is that it doesn't finish a job, never that it loses a photo.

- **It never deletes anything.** Deleting is switched off on the memory card and on the computer. She formats her card herself, and only after the morning report confirms both backups.
- **It copies and never moves.** The originals stay on the card the whole time. If the computer crashes or loses power, nothing is lost and it simply starts again.
- **It checks every file.** Each copy is compared to the original, for both backups, before the report says the session is safe.
- **Culling is only a marker.** Blinks and duplicates are labeled for her review and stay exactly where they are.
- **Uploading only reads.** Gallery uploads work from her exported files and never change anything in her folders.
- **When something looks wrong, it stops.** If a file won't copy, a count doesn't match, the drive isn't plugged in or the cloud drive is full, it stops and puts the issue at the top of the report.

## The Assistant at launch

The Assistant is a speed tool, not an effects machine. The photographs are what make the reel look beautiful, so the Assistant's job is to get a polished reel posted quickly, with no transitions, cinematic effects or other decoration that photographers don't use.

- **Song first.** She previews and picks the song, and the Assistant cuts her photos on the beat. Landing on the beat is what photographers care about most, and Dustin's test cut landed perfectly.
- **A few simple formats.** Behind the scenes into photos, photos only, B-roll, a behind-the-scenes sneak peek, or a client's DM testimonial with the photos flashing behind it. Few choices, so she picks one and posts.
- **Everything around the reel.** Several hook options, captions in her voice, stories for the day, and carousels built from clean, simple layouts such as one image scrolling horizontally across slides.

## The Concierge, the first new hire

- **The inbox.** A few times a day it checks for new inquiries, looks up who they are and finds their Instagram, drafts a reply in her voice, and taps her on the shoulder when it's ready to send.
- **Milestones.** It flags each session for the moments that matter, like a first birthday after a newborn session, and drafts a note at the right time.
- **Testimonials.** Two weeks after delivery it drafts a request for a review, and when a reply comes in, it saves it to her testimonials folder alongside the photos from that session.
- **Thank-you gifts.** It can find a florist near the family's home and suggest sending flowers. Any purchase waits for her approval.
- **Print and album offers.** When the family favorites images in their gallery, it drafts a print or album suggestion built around those exact favorites.

## What makes it even better

- **It learns from her changes.** When she rewrites a caption or softens a line in an email, it updates her Studio File, so within a month its drafts sound like her.
- **It checks the export before the client sees it.** It catches an unedited image, a blink that slipped through, a wrong export size, or a count that doesn't match.
- **It sends a Sunday night recap.** A few lines on what she shot, what she delivered, which gallery is due next and which clients are waiting on a reply.

## How it works

The Studio installs on the photographer's own computer, built so it can run long jobs like a full backup or a mini session sort without stopping to ask permission at every step. The install happens once, and Nicole teaches it step by step in short walkthrough videos, the same way she teaches everything else. Setup works like the Engine's Start Here interview: it learns how she names folders, which drive her sessions go to, what she promises clients, which apps she uses and how she sounds, and that becomes her Studio File.

Gmail, Google Calendar, Google Drive and Dropbox connect directly today. Pixieset has no connection for outside tools, so the Studio Manager works inside the Pixieset website the way she does, in her browser while she stays signed in, and it never handles her password. The same approach works for Pic-Time, ShootProof and other gallery platforms.

**Making it good is the real work.** Getting a first version to work is the easy part. Making every reel, email and report good enough that photographers happily pay for it takes testing, training and research into the best existing templates and tools. That is where most of the build time goes.

**Limits to plan around.** Her computer has to be on during the work, and her session drive has to be plugged in. Editing still happens in Lightroom or with an editing service, so the Studio prepares everything around the edit and never replaces her style.

## Decided

It launches to every photographer. Every photographer has to be able to finish setup on her own time, so Nicole teaches it through a short step-by-step video course alongside the setup interview inside the tool, and those videos double as launch content. Pixieset, Pic-Time and ShootProof need to work on day one. Safety testing happens before launch, on a handful of photographers' past sessions across different computers and gallery platforms.

The name keeps both ideas. It is the Studio Manager, because it works whenever a card goes in, day or night. The overnight picture lives in the marketing: "Plug in your card tonight and wake up to it done."

The Black Friday window is off the table. This is built to be good, not rushed.

## Build phases

- **Version 1:** The Studio Manager's backup, file checks, review markers, sorting several sessions on one card, sneak peeks, client note, calendar, gallery setup, export check and upload, plus the handoff to the Assistant and the Assistant's beat-cut reels, hooks, captions, stories and carousels. This is the full loop from card to post, and it is what every photographer will feel on day one.
- **Version 2:** The Concierge, learning from her edits, the Sunday recap, invoices and bookkeeping. The first new hire, sold as an add-on to Studio owners, with its own launch moment.
- **Version 3:** The Lighting Tech, the Creative Director and the full client journey through prints, albums and anniversary rebooking. More hires, each sold as an add-on with its own launch.

## Decisions still to make

- **Pricing.** What the Studio costs, what the Engine costs on its own, and what each new hire costs as an add-on. Photographers also need their own Claude plan, which is part of what they spend.
- **Updates.** Gallery platforms like Pixieset change their websites from time to time, which can mean the Studio Manager needs an update. Decide whether buyers get those updates free for a set time, or whether they come as a small paid update.
- **The product's name.** "The Studio" is a working name only.
- **Which booking systems to support at launch.** HoneyBook, Dubsado and similar tools, chosen by what photographers in your audience actually use.

## The first test

Pick one of Nicole's past sessions that is already safely backed up. Run version 1 on it from start to finish, then check every file, every count, every folder and every draft by hand. When it passes, try it on a live session with every step still waiting for approval.

A memory card going into a laptop at 9 PM. The lights go off. At 7 AM a cup of coffee sits next to the morning report, and Nicole taps approve. The gallery goes out, the Studio Manager asks whether to pass the favorites to the Assistant, and the reel is already cut on the beat. The whole story fits in fifteen seconds.
